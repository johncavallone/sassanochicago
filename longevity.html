Long-lived and short-lived families.

Another open question from the start of this project: do certain family lines live unusually long, or unusually short, lives? Here's what the tree shows once the comparison is done fairly.

The first attempt at this ran into an obvious trap: family lines with a lot of descendants who emigrated to Chicago looked dramatically longer-lived than everyone else — the "Rich" line (the Chicago-Americanized branch of Riccio) topped the list at a 73-year average, well clear of anyone else. But that's not a family trait. Most of that line lived their adult lives in 20th-century America with modern medicine and sanitation, not 18th- or 19th-century Sassano. Comparing that fairly against families who stayed in Italy for centuries isn't measuring genetics — it's measuring which families happened to catch a later, healthier era.

So the numbers below exclude anyone with a Chicago or Illinois connection entirely, comparing only people who lived and died in and around Sassano itself, and only counting adults — deaths at 15 or older, since infant and child mortality reflects a different set of causes than adult longevity does. Only surnames with at least 20 qualifying adult deaths are included, so a family of three isn't skewing the picture.

Against a baseline adult average of 58.2 years (median 62), the longest-lived line remains Miele at a 72.4-year average, backed by 32 qualifying adults. Delisa holds second. Borgia, which entered the rankings after crossing this page's 20-person minimum, holds third place at 68.6 years across 24 people — still a small sample, and one that can move a fair amount as more records arrive. Martucci, Caporrino and Pizzi round out the top six, with Stavola seventh. Stavola folds in a branch of the family recorded as "di Stavola" in some records — the same line under an older form of the surname, not a separate one, combined into a single 284-person figure. The shortest-lived is Curcio, at a 47.1-year average; Truglio and Baialardo follow close behind, then Esposito (52.0 years across 23 people), di Lorenzo (52.8 across 60), and finally Orlando and Gruccio at 53.1 and 53.3 (61 and 62 people), with Ricciardone, Bellezza and Petriello just behind. This update's movement was small and mostly in the middle of the table. Pizzi adds two adults (now 59) and its average eases from 64.0 to 63.8 years, which keeps it sixth, just ahead of Stavola, unchanged at 63.8. Setaro adds three (now 142) and edges up from 62.4 to 62.5 years, which lifts it past Biancamano (62.4) into twelfth; the two are within a tenth of a year of each other. Everything else in the top fifteen is unchanged. At the short-lived end, Vasquez adds three adults (now 77) and its average rises from 53.4 to 54.1 years, which lifts it out of the bottom-fifteen table altogether, and Picone (54.1 across 35 people) takes the fifteenth place it leaves; Ricciardone holds eighth at 53.4 across 91, and Barbella stays eleventh. The wider tree gained 41 adult lifespans, most of them in lines outside the two tables: Petrizzo adds eight and di Luca five. The overall adult average holds at 58.2 years across 12,691 lifespans, 41 more than last update, and the median stays at 62. (Last update, Pizzi added one adult and rose past Stavola, Biancamano eased to 62.4, and the bottom-fifteen table did not change.) The gap between the longest- and shortest-lived lines is unchanged at just over 25 years — a reminder that these rankings, especially near the bottom and around the edge of the top twelve, can still move as the tree grows.

A separate, harder question

Families with the highest child mortality.

This is a different measure entirely — average lifespan including infant and child deaths, which mostly reflects disease and circumstance rather than adult longevity. Several lines show a median age at death in single digits, meaning more than half the recorded deaths in that surname were children. This table was recomputed against the current tree using the same transparent method as the rest of this page (surnames with 20+ recorded lifespans); the previous version of this table used an older, undocumented methodology that couldn't be reconciled with the current data, so treat any comparison to a prior visit to this page with that in mind. This update the table's membership holds, with two moves. Battista adds three people and improves from 25.9 to 25.2 years across 99 people (48% of them children), which lifts it from seventh to fifth, past Sofia (25.3 across 31). Vasquez adds three and its average rises from 26.7 to 27.6 years across 157 people (42% of them children), so it slips from tenth to fifteenth. The rest: Esposito holds second at 24.0 years across 52 people, with Curcio (24.4 across 67, 40% of them children) third, Abbondanza seventh (25.8 across 57) and Ciorciari, which entered last update, eighth (26.3 across 43, 60% of them children). Cecere moves up to tenth (26.9 across 87), Cetrola eleventh (27.1 across 38) and Lombardo twelfth (27.1 years across 43 people, though only 30% of its recorded deaths were children). Caldacia sits thirteenth (27.2 across 34, 44% of them children) and Frabasile fourteenth (27.5 across 64). Caiafa (27.8 across 126), which dropped out last update when Ciorciari entered, stays just outside, and Conforti dropped out two updates ago, when it added an adult who lived to 58 and its average rose from 27.2 to 28.4 years across 25 people.

This table isn't restricted to Sassano-only records the way the two above are, since child mortality patterns are less likely to be confounded by the same era-of-emigration effect. Treat it as a starting point for which lines had the hardest time historically, not a genetic finding.

Sample sizes here are modest, and a handful of unlucky or lucky decades can shift a surname's average by several years. This is a reasonable first look at where to focus further research — not a conclusion about any family's health or genetics. As always, if you spot a name or record that looks off, let me know.
