Long-lived and short-lived families.

Another open question from the start of this project: do certain family lines live unusually long, or unusually short, lives? Here's what the tree shows once the comparison is done fairly.

The first attempt at this ran into an obvious trap: family lines with a lot of descendants who emigrated to Chicago looked dramatically longer-lived than everyone else — the "Rich" line (the Chicago-Americanized branch of Riccio) topped the list at a 73-year average, well clear of anyone else. But that's not a family trait. Most of that line lived their adult lives in 20th-century America with modern medicine and sanitation, not 18th- or 19th-century Sassano. Comparing that fairly against families who stayed in Italy for centuries isn't measuring genetics — it's measuring which families happened to catch a later, healthier era.

So the numbers below exclude anyone with a Chicago or Illinois connection entirely, comparing only people who lived and died in and around Sassano itself, and only counting adults — deaths at 15 or older, since infant and child mortality reflects a different set of causes than adult longevity does. Only surnames with at least 20 qualifying adult deaths are included, so a family of three isn't skewing the picture.

Against a baseline adult average of 58.2 years (median 62), the longest-lived line remains Miele at a 72.4-year average, backed by 32 qualifying adults. Delisa holds second. Borgia, which entered the rankings after crossing this page's 20-person minimum, holds third place at 68.6 years across 24 people — still a small sample, and one that can move a fair amount as more records arrive. Martucci, Caporrino and Pizzi round out the top six, with Stavola seventh. Stavola folds in a branch of the family recorded as "di Stavola" in some records — the same line under an older form of the surname, not a separate one, combined into a single 284-person figure. The shortest-lived is Curcio, at a 47.1-year average; Truglio and Baialardo follow close behind, then Esposito (52.0 years across 23 people), di Lorenzo (52.8 across 60), and finally Orlando and Gruccio at 53.1 and 53.3 (61 and 62 people), with Ricciardone, Bellezza and Barbella just behind. This update's movement was small and mostly in the middle of the table. Pizzi adds one adult (now 60) and its average edges up from 63.8 to 64.0 years, which keeps it sixth, just ahead of Stavola, unchanged at 63.8. Fornino adds two (now 118) and rises from 63.2 to 63.4 years, holding eighth, while d'Alessio nets five more adults (now 373) and its average eases from 62.2 to 61.9 years; that keeps it fourteenth, but only 0.1 of a year ahead of Bortone (61.8). Everything else in the top fifteen is unchanged. At the short-lived end, Petriello adds one adult (now 57) and its average rises from 53.5 to 54.1 years, which moves it from tenth to the fifteenth and last place in the bottom-fifteen table, just behind Picone (54.1 across 35 people); Barbella moves up to tenth, Ricciardone holds eighth at 53.4 across 91, and Vasquez, at 54.1 across 77, stays just outside the table. The wider tree gained 29 adult lifespans, most of them in lines outside the two tables: Ferro adds seven and d'Alessandro three. The overall adult average holds at 58.2 years across 12,720 lifespans, 29 more than last update, and the median stays at 62. (Last update, Pizzi added two adults and eased from 64.0 to 63.8 years, Setaro added three and edged past Biancamano into twelfth, and Vasquez added three adults and rose out of the bottom-fifteen table, with Picone taking the fifteenth place it left; the update before that, Pizzi added one adult and rose past Stavola, Biancamano eased to 62.4, and the bottom-fifteen table did not change.) The gap between the longest- and shortest-lived lines is unchanged at just over 25 years — a reminder that these rankings, especially near the bottom and around the edge of the top twelve, can still move as the tree grows.

A separate, harder question

Families with the highest child mortality.

This is a different measure entirely — average lifespan including infant and child deaths, which mostly reflects disease and circumstance rather than adult longevity. Several lines show a median age at death in single digits, meaning more than half the recorded deaths in that surname were children. This table was recomputed against the current tree using the same transparent method as the rest of this page (surnames with 20+ recorded lifespans); the previous version of this table used an older, undocumented methodology that couldn't be reconciled with the current data, so treat any comparison to a prior visit to this page with that in mind. This update the table's membership holds, with one move. Sofia adds two people and improves from 25.3 to 24.6 years across 33 people (48% of them children), which lifts it from sixth to fourth, past Crisci (24.7 across 41, 56% of them children) and Battista (25.2 across 99, 48% of them children), which slip to fifth and sixth. This table isn't restricted to Sassano-only records the way the two above are, since child mortality patterns are less likely to be confounded by the same era-of-emigration effect. Treat it as a starting point for which lines had the hardest time historically, not a genetic finding.

Sample sizes here are modest, and a handful of unlucky or lucky decades can shift a surname's average by several years. This is a reasonable first look at where to focus further research — not a conclusion about any family's health or genetics. As always, if you spot a name or record that looks off, let me know.
