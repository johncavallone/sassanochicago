Long-lived and short-lived families.

Another open question from the start of this project: do certain family lines live unusually long, or unusually short, lives? Here's what the tree shows once the comparison is done fairly.

The first attempt at this ran into an obvious trap: family lines with a lot of descendants who emigrated to Chicago looked dramatically longer-lived than everyone else — the "Rich" line (the Chicago-Americanized branch of Riccio) topped the list at a 73-year average, well clear of anyone else. But that's not a family trait. Most of that line lived their adult lives in 20th-century America with modern medicine and sanitation, not 18th- or 19th-century Sassano. Comparing that fairly against families who stayed in Italy for centuries isn't measuring genetics — it's measuring which families happened to catch a later, healthier era.

So the numbers below exclude anyone with a Chicago or Illinois connection entirely, comparing only people who lived and died in and around Sassano itself, and only counting adults — deaths at 15 or older, since infant and child mortality reflects a different set of causes than adult longevity does. Only surnames with at least 20 qualifying adult deaths are included, so a family of three isn't skewing the picture.

Against a baseline adult average of 58.3 years (median 62), the longest-lived line remains Miele at a 72.4-year average, backed by 32 qualifying adults. Delisa holds second. Borgia, which entered the rankings after crossing this page's 20-person minimum, holds third place at 68.6 years across 24 people — still a small sample, and one that can move a fair amount as more records arrive. Martucci, Caporrino and Stavola round out the top six, with Pizzi seventh. Stavola folds in a branch of the family recorded as "di Stavola" in some records — the same line under an older form of the surname, not a separate one, combined into a single 275-person figure. The shortest-lived is Curcio, at a 47.6-year average; Baialardo and Truglio follow close behind, then Esposito (52.0 years across 23 people), di Lorenzo (52.8 across 60), and finally Barbella, Orlando and Battista at 53.1 to 53.2 (53, 61 and 45 people), with Gruccio, Vasquez and Bellezza just behind. At the short-lived end, Truglio adds an adult (now 26) and its average drops from 50.5 to 50.3, which keeps it second shortest-lived, ahead of Baialardo (50.8 across 48). Further up the list, Bortone moves up a place to fifteenth (61.7 across 32) as Romano, which adds three adults (now 65), slips to sixteenth (61.7); d'Alessandro adds two (now 95) and falls from 22nd to 23rd (60.1), Cibelli rises to 22nd, d'Onza (157 adults) holds 27th, Petrizzo moves up to 29th (412 adults) and Pinto slips to 30th. The overall adult average holds at 58.3 years across 12,494 lifespans, 37 more than last update. The gap between the top and bottom of the list sits at just under 25 years — a reminder that these rankings, especially near the bottom, can still move as the tree grows.

A separate, harder question

Families with the highest child mortality.

This is a different measure entirely — average lifespan including infant and child deaths, which mostly reflects disease and circumstance rather than adult longevity. Several lines show a median age at death in single digits, meaning more than half the recorded deaths in that surname were children. This table was recomputed against the current tree using the same transparent method as the rest of this page (surnames with 20+ recorded lifespans); the previous version of this table used an older, undocumented methodology that couldn't be reconciled with the current data, so treat any comparison to a prior visit to this page with that in mind. This update Esposito holds third at 24.0 years across 52 people, and Abbondanza holds sixth (25.8 across 57) with Battista seventh (26.0 across 96, from 26.2 across 95). Lombardo holds tenth (26.9 years across 43 people, though only 30% of its recorded deaths were children) and Vasquez ninth (26.7 across 154). Cecere rises from fourteenth to eleventh (26.9 across 87), Cetrola slips to twelfth (27.1 across 38), and Caiafa returns to the bottom fifteen at fifteenth (27.9 across 123) as Tramutola drops out.

This table isn't restricted to Sassano-only records the way the two above are, since child mortality patterns are less likely to be confounded by the same era-of-emigration effect. Treat it as a starting point for which lines had the hardest time historically, not a genetic finding.

Sample sizes here are modest, and a handful of unlucky or lucky decades can shift a surname's average by several years. This is a reasonable first look at where to focus further research — not a conclusion about any family's health or genetics. As always, if you spot a name or record that looks off, let me know.
