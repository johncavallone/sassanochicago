Long-lived and short-lived families.

Another open question from the start of this project: do certain family lines live unusually long, or unusually short, lives? Here's what the tree shows once the comparison is done fairly.

The first attempt at this ran into an obvious trap: family lines with a lot of descendants who emigrated to Chicago looked dramatically longer-lived than everyone else — the "Rich" line (the Chicago-Americanized branch of Riccio) topped the list at a 73-year average, well clear of anyone else. But that's not a family trait. Most of that line lived their adult lives in 20th-century America with modern medicine and sanitation, not 18th- or 19th-century Sassano. Comparing that fairly against families who stayed in Italy for centuries isn't measuring genetics — it's measuring which families happened to catch a later, healthier era.

So the numbers below exclude anyone with a Chicago or Illinois connection entirely, comparing only people who lived and died in and around Sassano itself, and only counting adults — deaths at 15 or older, since infant and child mortality reflects a different set of causes than adult longevity does. Only surnames with at least 20 qualifying adult deaths are included, so a family of three isn't skewing the picture.

Against a baseline adult average of 58.2 years (median 62), the longest-lived line remains Miele at a 72.4-year average, backed by 32 qualifying adults. Delisa holds second. Borgia, which entered the rankings after crossing this page's 20-person minimum, holds third place at 68.6 years across 24 people — still a small sample, and one that can move a fair amount as more records arrive. Martucci, Caporrino and Pizzi round out the top six, with Stavola seventh. Stavola folds in a branch of the family recorded as "di Stavola" in some records — the same line under an older form of the surname, not a separate one, combined into a single 284-person figure. The shortest-lived is Curcio, at a 47.1-year average; Truglio and Baialardo follow close behind, then di Lorenzo (52.4 years across 59 people), Esposito (52.7 across 24), and finally Orlando, Battista and Gruccio at 53.1, 53.3 and 53.3 (61, 45 and 62 people), with Ricciardone, Bellezza and Barbella just behind. A separate, harder question

Families with the highest child mortality.

This is a different measure entirely — average lifespan including infant and child deaths, which mostly reflects disease and circumstance rather than adult longevity. Several lines show a median age at death in single digits, meaning more than half the recorded deaths in that surname were children. This table was recomputed against the current tree using the same transparent method as the rest of this page (surnames with 20+ recorded lifespans); the previous version of this table used an older, undocumented methodology that couldn't be reconciled with the current data, so treat any comparison to a prior visit to this page with that in mind. This table isn't restricted to Sassano-only records the way the two above are, since child mortality patterns are less likely to be confounded by the same era-of-emigration effect. Treat it as a starting point for which lines had the hardest time historically, not a genetic finding.

Sample sizes here are modest, and a handful of unlucky or lucky decades can shift a surname's average by several years. This is a reasonable first look at where to focus further research — not a conclusion about any family's health or genetics. As always, if you spot a name or record that looks off, let me know.
