Long-lived and short-lived families.

Another open question from the start of this project: do certain family lines live unusually long, or unusually short, lives? Here's what the tree shows once the comparison is done fairly.

The first attempt at this ran into an obvious trap: family lines with a lot of descendants who emigrated to Chicago looked dramatically longer-lived than everyone else — the "Rich" line (the Chicago-Americanized branch of Riccio) topped the list at a 73-year average, well clear of anyone else. But that's not a family trait. Most of that line lived their adult lives in 20th-century America with modern medicine and sanitation, not 18th- or 19th-century Sassano. Comparing that fairly against families who stayed in Italy for centuries isn't measuring genetics — it's measuring which families happened to catch a later, healthier era.

So the numbers below exclude anyone with a Chicago or Illinois connection entirely, comparing only people who lived and died in and around Sassano itself, and only counting adults — deaths at 15 or older, since infant and child mortality reflects a different set of causes than adult longevity does. Only surnames with at least 20 qualifying adult deaths are included, so a family of three isn't skewing the picture.

Against a baseline adult average of 59.9 years (median 64), the longest-lived line is now Miele at a 72.4-year average — a genuine change from the last time this was run, when di Stefano led at 66.6. Miele's rise is backed by real volume, 30 qualifying adults, not a fluke of two or three lucky lifespans, which makes it a stronger claim than the number it replaced. di Stefano, Martucci, Stavola, and Caporrino round out the rest of the top five. The shortest-lived is still Battista at 49.2 years, with Baialardo, di Lorenzo, Gruccio, and Petriello close behind. The gap between the top and bottom of the list has actually widened with this update, from 17 years to just over 23 — worth treating as a lead to watch rather than a settled ranking, since a single surname's position can still shift as more records come in.

This table isn't restricted to Sassano-only records the way the two above are, since child mortality patterns are less likely to be confounded by the same era-of-emigration effect. Treat it as a starting point for which lines had the hardest time historically, not a genetic finding.

Sample sizes here are modest, and a handful of unlucky or lucky decades can shift a surname's average by several years. This is a reasonable first look at where to focus further research — not a conclusion about any family's health or genetics. As always, if you spot a name or record that looks off, let me know.
