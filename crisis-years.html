Four spikes across 380 years · 25,912 individuals

Crisis years.

Four times in the tree's history, deaths spike sharply above the ordinary background rate: 1816–1818, 1837, 1862, and 1872. Each has its own story — a documented famine, a global pandemic, a civil war, and one that's still an open question. See Notable Records for the largest families, youngest mothers and brides, and other extremes across the tree.

1816–1818

The years the tree remembers a famine.

Between 1815 and 1819, a volcanic eruption at Mount Tambora in Indonesia — the largest in recorded history — cooled the global climate enough to trigger widespread crop failure across Europe. 1816 became known as "The Year Without a Summer." Famine followed, and where famine goes, typhus and other disease follow close behind. Southern Italy was hit hard. In August 2021, Giuseppina Dede of the Museo del Cognome in Padula, Italy — a town just down the road from Sassano — independently confirmed this from her own records: a typhus fever epidemic, tifo petecchiale, struck the area in 1817. That's a genuine, unprompted match to what the tree already showed.

The tree shows it clearly, without needing to be told the history first: deaths jump from a baseline of about 88 a year to 206 a year on average across 1816–1818 — a 2.35× spike, essentially unchanged across twenty-seven tree updates now. 1817 alone recorded 378 deaths, by far the worst single year anywhere in the tree's 380-year span. The town's own parish population records tell the same story at an even sharper edge: against a population of roughly 3,700, 1817 saw 389 church deaths — a 10.5% death rate in a single year, more than triple the surrounding decades' norm.

Breaking those 619 deaths down by age at death tells its own story: 341 (55%) were adults (15 and older), 207 (33%) were children between 1 and 14, and 71 (11%) were infants under a year old. This was a crisis that reached every generation of a household at once — it didn't spare working-age adults to take only the very young, the way some epidemics do. Against the 1795–1815 baseline, the shift is real but modest: normal years ran 49% adult, 33% child, 18% infant, so the crisis skewed a little further toward adults and a little away from infants — not a dramatically different mortality profile, just a much bigger version of the ordinary one.

The hardest-hit surname is Calandriello (26 deaths in the three-year window), followed by d'Alessio and d'Amato (24 and 23). Eighty-nine individual families lost two or more children within the same three years.

No single family shows the full weight of that famine better than Isidoro d'Amato's. Between April and December 1817 alone, the crisis took nine members of his extended family across three generations: two of his siblings (Rosaria Giovanna in April, Donato Antonio in May), his wife Albabella Cusati (August), two of his adult children (Vincenzo Ferdinando in November, Rosa Maria in December), three of his grandchildren (a one-year-old and an eight-year-old among them) — and finally Isidoro himself, on 30 November, at 66. Nine deaths in one household, spanning nearly the entire year, is about as complete a picture of what this crisis actually did to a family as the records offer anywhere in the tree.

Two other households show the same crisis from different angles. Giuseppe Gennaro Russo & Angelica Burzo lost five adult children in barely two years — the largest single-generation loss found — though their line continued, since most of those five had already had children of their own. And Cono Caiafa & Giacomina d'Onza lost three of their children within an 11-week span in mid-to-late 1817, with Giacomina herself dying in the middle of it, on 27 Aug, not yet 40 — a household that lost its mother alongside its children, not just its children.

1837

A second crisis, twenty years later.

The 1816–1818 typhus crisis wasn't the only epidemic the records remember. The San Giovanni Evangelista Defunctorum (death) register notes a cholera outbreak in 1837 — part of the second global cholera pandemic, which reached the Kingdom of Naples that year. The tree bears this out too: deaths in 1837 run 193, essentially flat with the last two updates, against a baseline of about 108 a year across the surrounding decade — a 1.79× spike.

The age breakdown skews even further toward adults than 1817 did: 135 of the 193 deaths (70%) were adults, against 35 (18%) children and 23 (12%) infants. That fits cholera's known profile — unlike a slow famine, it kills previously healthy adults just as readily as the very young, since it spreads through contaminated water and strikes fast regardless of age. The baseline comparison makes this sharper still: in ordinary years (1825–1836), adults made up 45% of deaths, children 33%, and infants 22% — so 1837 didn't just add more deaths, it inverted the usual pattern, pulling hard toward adults and well away from both children and infants.

One family shows exactly how sudden and total that kind of outbreak could be. Francesco Antonio Matteo di Sisti and Agnese Venezia Lefante, married 3 May 1802, had eight recorded children. Three of them — Vincenzo, Giuseppe, and Maria — all died on the same day, 15 July 1837, per the parish death register, entries No. 56–58. Adult siblings, living in the same household, dying together within hours of one another, is close to a textbook signature of cholera, which can kill within a day of the first symptoms. Francesco himself had already died in 1833 and didn't live to see it; Agnese outlived all three of these children, dying in 1846.

A second household shows the same year from another angle. Domenico Russo, married to Maria di Novella since 15 Jan 1824, died 10 Jul 1837 at 29 — just five days before the di Sisti siblings. Two of his young daughters didn't survive the year either: Arcangela, not yet 5, died a month before him on 11 Jun 1837; Rosa, only 11 months old, died two months after him on 18 Sep 1837. Father and two daughters, all gone within a single season — a household where the crisis returned more than once rather than striking all at once.

1817 and 1837, twenty years apart

Families hit by both crises.

Most families that appear in both this page's crisis years are there by coincidence — two unrelated bad years, two unrelated losses. Two families in the tree don't fit that pattern: each lost members to the 1817 typhus crisis, then lost more to the 1837 cholera outbreak two decades later.

Giovanni Paolo Antonio Chiurillo (1714–1787) didn't live to see either crisis, but three of his children did, and none of them survived 1817: Angela (1746–1817, d. 4 Jul), Teresa (1751–1817, d. 23 Jul), and Francesco Rosario (1759–1817, d. 24 Aug) all died within seven weeks of each other that year. Twenty years later, the crisis found the family again. A fourth child, Felicita Antonia (1763–1837, d. 5 Aug), died in the 1837 outbreak — and so did her late brother Francesco Rosario's own widow, Giovanna Rubino (1764–1837, d. 25 Jul), and their son Michele (1804–1837, d. 28 Jul). Giovanna and Michele died just three days apart, and Felicita followed a week after that — three more losses within two weeks of each other, in the same family that had already buried three siblings in 1817. Six members of one family, across two crises twenty years apart.

Francesco Antonio di Lisa (1767–1817) died on 15 Sep 1817; his son Giovanni (1814–1817) followed just two days later, on 17 Sep — father and infant son, days apart. Twenty years on, in 1837, another son, Michele di Lisa (1802–1837, d. 4 Aug), was gone too.

1862 & 1872

Two more spikes, further along.

The tree shows two more mortality spikes later in the 19th century, first noticed in the Stato Civile death records: 207 deaths in 1862, roughly double the surrounding decade's average of about 102 a year, and 202 deaths in 1872, close to 1.9 times the roughly 108-a-year norm of the years around it. 1872's count has now climbed in four updates running, from 162 to 175 to 191 to 202 — closing almost all the way in on 1862's total, which has stayed comparatively flat. Neither year lines up with any of Italy's officially recognized national cholera pandemic years — 1835–37, 1849, 1854–55, 1865–67, 1884–86, and 1893 — so unlike 1817 and 1837, these two don't have an obvious epidemic to point to. That sent us looking for a different kind of explanation, and we found one for 1862, though 1872 is still an open question.

1862 lines up instead with a war, not a disease. After Italian unification in 1861, the former Kingdom of the Two Sicilies erupted into brigantaggio — a brutal, years-long insurgency and military crackdown that some historians consider closer to a civil war than banditry. A source specifically covering Sala Consilina and the Vallo di Diano — Sassano's own district — documents brigantaggio activity and military reprisals, including executions, running through exactly 1861 and 1862. The tree's own data lines up with that timeline: 1861 was already elevated before 1862's spike, consistent with unrest building through 1861 and peaking the following year. It's a plausible, geographically specific explanation — though, unlike 1817's typhus or 1837's cholera, this is a spike we're attributing to violence and its consequences rather than to a documented disease outbreak, and it deserves the same caveat any historical inference does: it's the best-supported explanation found so far, not a certainty. One thing worth flagging against it: the age breakdown doesn't obviously fit a war story. Of 1862's 207 deaths, 112 (54%) were children aged 1–14, against 71 (34%) adults and 24 (12%) infants — a far more child-heavy year than either 1817 or 1837, where armed conflict and reprisals would more typically be expected to fall hardest on adults. The baseline makes this starker: in ordinary years nearby (1855–1861), adults made up 68% of deaths and children only 20% — almost the exact reverse of 1862's own numbers. That's not a modest skew, it's a flipped mortality profile, and it sits uneasily next to a war-and-reprisals explanation. That doesn't rule brigantaggio out — disrupted harvests, displacement, and breakdowns in care during unrest can hit children hardest too — but it's a genuine tension in the evidence, not a clean fit.

1872 is less settled. The years around it don't match a recognized Italian cholera wave either, but they do fall within the Great Smallpox Pandemic that swept Europe from about 1870 to 1875, following troop movements from the Franco-Prussian War. The sources documenting that pandemic's severity focus mainly on Northern and Central Europe, and we haven't found a source confirming smallpox specifically in Salerno province or Sassano for 1872 — so this is offered as the most plausible lead, not a confirmed cause. If a parish or civil record ever turns up a cause of death for that year, it would settle the question either way. Here the age data leans the other way: 107 of 202 deaths (53%) were children, against 63 (31%) adults and 32 (16%) infants — a profile that fits smallpox reasonably well, since outbreaks of the disease have historically hit children hardest in populations where most adults had already been exposed and carried some immunity from an earlier wave. Compared to the 1865–1871 baseline — 59% adult, 27% child, 14% infant in ordinary years — 1872 shows the same reversal seen in 1862: a normally adult-heavy mortality pattern flipping hard toward children, with a rising infant share too. Two consecutive "unexplained" spikes sharing the same unusual age signature is itself worth noting, whatever turns out to have caused each one individually.
