14 spikes across 380 years · 26,538 individuals

Crisis years.

Fourteen times in the tree's history, deaths spike sharply above the ordinary background rate — not just the four this page is best known for. 1817, 1837, 1862, and 1872 get the deepest treatment below, each with its own story: a documented famine, a global pandemic, a civil war, and one that's still an open question. Ten more spikes, covered further down the page, range from a well-documented Kingdom-of-Naples-wide famine and a second Tambora-style eruption to a couple that remain genuinely unexplained. See Notable Records for the largest families, youngest mothers and brides, and other extremes across the tree.

1817

The year the tree remembers a famine.

Between 1815 and 1819, a volcanic eruption at Mount Tambora in Indonesia — the largest in recorded history — cooled the global climate enough to trigger widespread crop failure across Europe. 1816 became known as "The Year Without a Summer." Famine followed, and where famine goes, typhus and other disease follow close behind. Southern Italy was hit hard. In August 2021, Giuseppina Dede of the Museo del Cognome in Padula, Italy — a town just down the road from Sassano — independently confirmed this from her own records: a typhus fever epidemic, tifo petecchiale, struck the area in 1817. That's a genuine, unprompted match to what the tree already showed.

A closer look at the year-by-year numbers shows the spike is narrower than it first appears. Against a baseline of about 90 deaths a year across 1795–1815, 1816 (119 deaths, 1.33×) and 1818 (129 deaths, 1.44×) both sit inside the ordinary range of year-to-year variation — no different, really, from other unremarkable years nearby like 1810 (1.66×) or 1815 (1.36×). 1817 alone is the true outlier: 379 deaths, 4.22× the baseline, and by a wide margin the worst single year anywhere in the tree's 380-year span. What briefly looked like a three-year crisis turns out, on closer inspection, to be one catastrophic year bracketed by two ordinary ones — so this page treats 1817 on its own, not as part of a wider 1816–1818 window. The town's own parish population records tell the same concentrated story at an even sharper edge: against a population of roughly 3,700, 1817 saw 389 church deaths — a 10.5% death rate in a single year, more than triple the surrounding decades' norm.

Breaking 1817's 379 deaths down by age at death: 194 (51%) were adults (15 and older), 142 (38%) were children between 1 and 14, and 43 (11%) were infants under a year old. This was a crisis that reached every generation of a household at once — it didn't spare working-age adults to take only the very young, the way some epidemics do. Against the 1795–1815 baseline (49% adult, 33% child, 18% infant), the shift is real but modest: 1817 skewed a little further toward adults and children and a little away from infants — not a dramatically different mortality profile, just a much bigger version of the ordinary one.

The hardest-hit surname is Calandriello (19 deaths in 1817 alone), followed by d'Alessio (16) and d'Amato (15). Forty individual families lost two or more children within that single year.

No single family shows the full weight of that famine better than Isidoro d'Amato's. Between April and December 1817 alone, the crisis took nine members of his extended family across three generations: two of his siblings (Rosaria Giovanna in April, Donato Antonio in May), his wife Albabella Cusati (August), two of his adult children (Vincenzo Ferdinando in November, Rosa Maria in December), three of his grandchildren (a one-year-old and an eight-year-old among them) — and finally Isidoro himself, on 30 November, at 66. All nine deaths fall within calendar-year 1817 — nothing here bleeds into the neighboring, unremarkable years either side of it. Nine deaths in one household in a single year is about as complete a picture of what this crisis actually did to a family as the records offer anywhere in the tree.

Two other households show the same single, brutal year from different angles. Rocco Pellegrino & Gaetana Vasquez lost five members of their household — both parents and three of their children — entirely within 1817: Rocco himself on 9 February, their son Giuseppe (6) on 3 September, daughter Erminia (2) on 11 September, Gaetana on 10 October, and daughter Giacomina (10) a week later on 17 October. Of their six recorded children, only one, Maria, survived the year — she lived on to 1871. And Cono Caiafa & Giacomina d'Onza lost three of their children within an 11-week span in mid-to-late 1817, with Giacomina herself dying in the middle of it, on 27 Aug, not yet 40 — a household that lost its mother alongside its children, not just its children, all inside that same single year.

Not every family's hardest stretch lines up quite so neatly with the town-wide numbers, and one household is worth including precisely because it doesn't. Giuseppe Gennaro Russo & Angelica Burzo lost five of their adult children — Gioacchino Michele (63), Vincenzo (55), Francesca Catarina (42), Salvatore Gesualdo (67), and Santoro (61) — across barely more than two years, but only two of those five deaths, Francesca Catarina's in February and Salvatore Gesualdo's in August, actually fall in 1817 itself; the other three fall in 1816 and 1818, the two years the town-wide data now shows as ordinary. Five siblings, all already well into middle age or older, dying in close succession across three consecutive years reads less like a town-wide catastrophe reaching this one family and more like a family with some shared vulnerability of its own — age, a shared household, fragile health passed among them — that happened to unfold alongside the wider crisis rather than because of it. It's a useful reminder that the aggregate data drawing a clean line around 1817 doesn't mean every family's own worst years lined up inside it.

1837

A second crisis, twenty years later.

The 1817 typhus crisis wasn't the only epidemic the records remember. The San Giovanni Evangelista Defunctorum (death) register notes a cholera outbreak in 1837 — part of the second global cholera pandemic, which reached the Kingdom of Naples that year. The tree bears this out too: deaths in 1837 sit at 194, essentially flat for the eighth update running, against a baseline of about 114 a year across the surrounding decade — a 1.71× spike.

The age breakdown skews even further toward adults than 1817 did: 136 of the 194 deaths (70%) were adults, against 35 (18%) children and 23 (12%) infants. That fits cholera's known profile — unlike a slow famine, it kills previously healthy adults just as readily as the very young, since it spreads through contaminated water and strikes fast regardless of age. The baseline comparison makes this sharper still: in ordinary years (1825–1836), adults made up 44% of deaths, children 34%, and infants 22% — so 1837 didn't just add more deaths, it inverted the usual pattern, pulling hard toward adults and well away from both children and infants.

One family shows exactly how sudden and total that kind of outbreak could be. Francesco Antonio Matteo di Sisti and Agnese Venezia Lefante, married 3 May 1802, had eight recorded children. Three of them — Vincenzo, Giuseppe, and Maria — all died on the same day, 15 July 1837, per the parish death register, entries No. 56–58. Adult siblings, living in the same household, dying together within hours of one another, is close to a textbook signature of cholera, which can kill within a day of the first symptoms. Francesco himself had already died in 1833 and didn't live to see it; Agnese outlived all three of these children, dying in 1846.

A second household shows the same year from another angle. Domenico Russo, married to Maria di Novella since 15 Jan 1824, died 10 Jul 1837 at 29 — just five days before the di Sisti siblings. Two of his young daughters didn't survive the year either: Arcangela, not yet 5, died a month before him on 11 Jun 1837; Rosa, only 11 months old, died two months after him on 18 Sep 1837. Father and two daughters, all gone within a single season — a household where the crisis returned more than once rather than striking all at once.

1817 and 1837, twenty years apart

Families hit by both crises.

Most families that appear in both this page's crisis years are there by coincidence — two unrelated bad years, two unrelated losses. Two families in the tree don't fit that pattern: each lost members to the 1817 typhus crisis, then lost more to the 1837 cholera outbreak two decades later.

Giovanni Paolo Antonio Chiurillo (1714–1787) didn't live to see either crisis, but three of his children did, and none of them survived 1817: Angela (1746–1817, d. 4 Jul), Teresa (1751–1817, d. 23 Jul), and Francesco Rosario (1759–1817, d. 24 Aug) all died within seven weeks of each other that year. Twenty years later, the crisis found the family again. A fourth child, Felicita Antonia (1763–1837, d. 5 Aug), died in the 1837 outbreak — and so did her late brother Francesco Rosario's own widow, Giovanna Rubino (1764–1837, d. 25 Jul), and their son Michele (1804–1837, d. 28 Jul). Giovanna and Michele died just three days apart, and Felicita followed a week after that — three more losses within two weeks of each other, in the same family that had already buried three siblings in 1817. Six members of one family, across two crises twenty years apart.

Francesco Antonio di Lisa (1767–1817) died on 15 Sep 1817; his son Giovanni (1814–1817) followed just two days later, on 17 Sep — father and infant son, days apart. Twenty years on, in 1837, another son, Michele di Lisa (1802–1837, d. 4 Aug), was gone too.

Beyond the headline four

Ten more spikes worth a second look.

Scanning the full 1750–1900 chart below turns up more than just 1817, 1837, 1862, and 1872. Ten other years stand out against their own local baseline: 1760, 1764, 1773, 1784, 1786, 1810, 1825, 1834, 1844, and 1851. Each was checked against the historical record for the Kingdom of Naples, with the same honesty about confidence level as the four crisis years get elsewhere on this page — some are as well documented as 1817 itself, some are plausible but unconfirmed for this specific area, and a few turned up nothing at all.

Three are strongly documented. 1764 lines up with one of the worst famines in the Kingdom of Naples' history — the 1763–64 famine killed roughly 200,000 people, some 5% of the entire kingdom's population, in 1764 alone, driven by catastrophic harvests and a feudal grain-distribution system that made things worse. The tree's own age profile fits: 72% of 1764's deaths were adults, only 18% children — a similar "hits every generation, skews adult" signature to 1817's own 51% adult / 38% child split. 1810 turns out to have its own Tambora: a major volcanic eruption in 1809, still geographically unidentified today but roughly half of Tambora's magnitude and one of the largest sulfate-emission events since 1500, caused significant global cooling through 1809–1811, with climate reconstructions specifically flagging a temperature low point over Southern Europe in 1810 — a second Tambora-style climate shock, seven years before the one already documented on this page. 1773 sits inside a documented grain "dearth" running 1773–1775, part of a broader European crisis that also produced France's 1775 Flour War riots; the sourcing is strongest for Northern Italy specifically, but the timing fits.

Four are plausible but unconfirmed for this area. 1784 and 1786 follow the Laki volcanic eruption in Iceland (June 1783–February 1784), which caused well-documented excess mortality and multi-year crop failures across Europe — poor harvests are specifically recorded in France for 1785, 1787, and 1788. No source found ties this directly to the Kingdom of Naples, but the timing brackets both years plausibly. 1834 and 1851 both fall inside the broad international cholera pandemic windows (the second pandemic ran 1826–49, the third 1846–60), but neither matches the specific, well-documented Two Sicilies outbreak dates of 1836–37 and 1854–55. 1851 remains the largest spike of all ten years by a wide margin — 213 deaths in the tree against a baseline of roughly 116, and 332 church deaths against that same baseline, a 2.87× spike well beyond even 1862 or 1872.

Three turned up nothing specific. 1760, 1844, and — before a closer look, described below — 1825 didn't match any documented crisis for the Kingdom of Naples in the sources checked. They may be local, Sassano-specific bad years that never made it into the broader historical record, or simply gaps in what's searchable today.

1825, a closer look

A ten-week autumn, not a bad year.

1825 didn't match anything in the historical record — but the tree's own data turned out to have a clearer story than any outside source could offer. Of the year's 185 deaths with a recorded month, 59% fall in just three months: October, November, and December. July recorded none at all. The other nine months of the year look like an entirely ordinary year in Sassano.

The age profile inside that ten-week window is the clearest signal on the page. Deaths from October through December were 74% children aged 1–14, against just 8% adults. Outside that window, across the rest of the year, the split looks completely different: 30% children, 38% adults — much closer to a normal year's mix. That reversal — a fast-moving window that hits children hard and mostly spares adults, then vanishes — doesn't read like a famine, which tends to build gradually and take a broader toll. It reads like a single infectious disease sweeping through in one season. The classic candidates for that profile in this era — measles, whooping cough, diphtheria, scarlet fever — all tend to cluster in autumn and winter and hit children disproportionately. No source found names a specific documented outbreak in the Kingdom of Naples for exactly this year, so this can't be given a name — but the internal pattern here is about as clean a signature as a record like this can produce.

Seventeen families lost two or more children in that window alone — a similar scale of clustering to 1817's worst individual households. The hardest hit: Francesco Romaniello & Arcangela Arnone lost four children in just over three weeks. Three of them — Michele, Candeloro, and Giovanni — died within a single day of each other, 19–20 October; the fourth, Loreto, followed three weeks later on 8 November.

1862 & 1872

Two more spikes, further along.

The tree shows two more mortality spikes later in the 19th century, first noticed in the Stato Civile death records: 223 deaths in 1862, roughly double the surrounding decade's average of about 106 a year, and 263 deaths in 1872, a bit over twice the roughly 113-a-year norm of the years around it. 1872 has now pulled well ahead of 1862 for a fifth update running, holding steady while 1862 also held — a real, sustained reversal from earlier tree versions, when 1862 was consistently the larger of the two. Neither year lines up with any of Italy's officially recognized national cholera pandemic years — 1835–37, 1849, 1854–55, 1865–67, 1884–86, and 1893 — so unlike 1817 and 1837, these two don't have an obvious epidemic to point to. That sent us looking for a different kind of explanation, and we found one for 1862, though 1872 is still an open question.

1862 lines up instead with a war, not a disease. After Italian unification in 1861, the former Kingdom of the Two Sicilies erupted into brigantaggio — a brutal, years-long insurgency and military crackdown that some historians consider closer to a civil war than banditry. A source specifically covering Sala Consilina and the Vallo di Diano — Sassano's own district — documents brigantaggio activity and military reprisals, including executions, running through exactly 1861 and 1862. The tree's own data lines up with that timeline: 1861 was already elevated before 1862's spike, consistent with unrest building through 1861 and peaking the following year. It's a plausible, geographically specific explanation — though, unlike 1817's typhus or 1837's cholera, this is a spike we're attributing to violence and its consequences rather than to a documented disease outbreak, and it deserves the same caveat any historical inference does: it's the best-supported explanation found so far, not a certainty. One thing worth flagging against it: the age breakdown doesn't obviously fit a war story. Of 1862's 223 deaths, 125 (56%) were children aged 1–14, against 72 (32%) adults and 26 (12%) infants — a far more child-heavy year than either 1817 or 1837, where armed conflict and reprisals would more typically be expected to fall hardest on adults. The baseline makes this starker: in ordinary years nearby (1855–1861), adults made up 66% of deaths and children only 21% — almost the exact reverse of 1862's own numbers. That's not a modest skew, it's a flipped mortality profile, and it sits uneasily next to a war-and-reprisals explanation. That doesn't rule brigantaggio out — disrupted harvests, displacement, and breakdowns in care during unrest can hit children hardest too — but it's a genuine tension in the evidence, not a clean fit.

1872 is less settled. The years around it don't match a recognized Italian cholera wave either, but they do fall within the Great Smallpox Pandemic that swept Europe from about 1870 to 1875, following troop movements from the Franco-Prussian War. The sources documenting that pandemic's severity focus mainly on Northern and Central Europe, and we haven't found a source confirming smallpox specifically in Salerno province or Sassano for 1872 — so this is offered as the most plausible lead, not a confirmed cause. If a parish or civil record ever turns up a cause of death for that year, it would settle the question either way. Here the age data leans the other way: 145 of 263 deaths (55%) were children, against 72 (27%) adults and 46 (17%) infants — again the highest infant share of any of the four crisis years on this page. That profile fits smallpox reasonably well, since outbreaks of the disease have historically hit children hardest in populations where most adults had already been exposed and carried some immunity from an earlier wave, though the elevated infant toll suggests a second, compounding strain on the youngest children specifically. Compared to the 1865–1871 baseline — 58% adult, 27% child, 14% infant in ordinary years — 1872 shows the same reversal seen in 1862: a normally adult-heavy mortality pattern flipping hard toward children, with infant deaths running well above their own baseline too.
