Which families married which.

Cousin marriage told us how closely related couples tended to be. This is a different question: regardless of blood relation, which surnames kept choosing each other, generation after generation?

Two surnames sit at the center of nearly everything: Calandriello and d'Amato. Between them they appear in a huge share of the tree's marriages — Calandriello alone shows up in 521 marriages across 106 different partner surnames, d'Amato in 505 across 119. Calandriello and Petrizzo hold at 26 marriages, still the single most common alliance documented anywhere in the tree, and the top of the table did not move this update: Calandriello and d'Amato stay at 24, Calandriello and Rubino at 21, d'Amato and Russo at 15, and Calandriello and Pinto and d'Alessio and d'Amato at 14. The number of marriages counted rose from 6,315 to 6,336 as new records were added, and the number of distinct surname pairings from 3,179 to 3,184. Among the individual surnames, Calandriello holds at 521 marriages across 106 partners and Petrizzo, third, at 417 across 102, while d'Amato adds five (now 505 across 119). Russo adds three and a partner surname (273 across 93), Carlo adds two (232 across 82), and Pinto (226 across 83), di Miele (236), Pellegrino (224), di Luca (202) and Libretti (197 across 93) each add one or two. Rubino is the only surname in the top twenty to lose a marriage, slipping from 263 to 262 and staying eighth. Same-surname marriages rise by one to 100, the new one being a Romano-to-Romano marriage, which lifts Romano to two; the leaders are unchanged, with Calandriello on 16, d'Amato on 12, Petrizzo on 8 and di Brizzi on 6.

That's not a coincidence of a couple of prolific individuals — it shows up at every level. Calandriello is also the most common marriage partner for a wide swath of major surnames — di Brizzi, Rubino, Arnone, Pinto, Russo, di Bella, Libretti, Ferro — meaning if a family in Sassano married outside its own name, Calandriello was disproportionately likely to be on the other side of that marriage. d'Amato plays the same role for Feminella, Pellegrino, Trotta, and Florendino. Between them, these two families functioned as something like the connective tissue of the whole town's marriage network — the families everyone else was likely to marry into sooner or later.

Same-surname marriages — two people who already shared a family name marrying each other, which in a town this size usually means some degree of relation even before you trace the exact connection — happened 100 times across the tree. Calandriello leads that list too, with 16, followed by d'Amato at 12 and Petrizzo at 8. For the exact blood relationships behind marriages like these, see the cousin marriage analysis — the two pages are looking at the same underlying pattern from different angles.

Worth noting: almost none of the top surname pairings show a Chicago connection directly — these are overwhelmingly historical, Sassano-based alliances from before the migration, which makes sense given how much deeper the tree's Italian branches run compared to its American ones.
