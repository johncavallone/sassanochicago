Anyone who has spent time in the parish books of San Giovanni Evangelista learns the same lesson we did while indexing them: in a town the size of Sassano, tucked in the hills 95 miles southeast of Naples, everyone eventually married a relative. Not out of scandal, but out of geography. For centuries the town's marriageable population was, quite literally, whoever lived within walking distance — and more often than not, that meant a cousin.

Using the full Sassano-Bompietro family tree — now over 30,309 individuals and 7,653 families as new records keep getting indexed — we set out to see just how common this really was, and whether it followed the Sassanesi all the way to Federal Street.

It did, and more than we expected.

Of the 6,120 marriages in the tree where both spouses' parentage could be traced, 1,309 were between blood relatives close enough to identify: first cousins, second cousins, third cousins, or fourth cousins, some with a generation's remove between them. The overall share edges up to 21.39%, from 21.37% last update. Ten cousin marriages joined the count and none left it, taking the total from 1,299 to 1,309, while new families added to the tree raised the number of marriages checked from 6,078 to 6,120. Seven of the ten are fourth-cousin matches, most of them involving the Petrizzo and Trotta families: Antonio Petrizzo and Francesca Trotta (19 Mar 1904) and Vincenzo Trotta and Maria Antonia Petrizzo (30 Dec 1905) both trace to Mattia Pasquale Petrizzo. The others in that group are Michele Ferro and Angela Calandriello (6 Apr 1877), Ambrosio Petrizzo and Barbara d'Amato (1 Mar 1883), Clemente Capuano and Giustina Riccio (9 Mar 1882), Michele Giordano and Maria Giuseppa Petrizzo (1 Oct 1886) and Domenico Lefante and Maria Giordano (15 Oct 1906). Two are third cousins once removed, Rosario Trotta and Emiddia Pellegrino (11 Oct 1877) and Angelo Maria Trotta and Rosa Ricciardone (3 Feb 1901), and one is a first cousin once removed, Alessandro Giovanni di Luca and Maria Trotta (21 Jan 1839), through Francesco di Luca. Fourth cousins rise by seven, to 451, third cousins once removed by two, to 332, and first cousins once removed by one, to 26. The first-cousin count holds at 33, and multi-generational cases rise to 871.

What surprised us more was how often it repeated. 871 people in the tree are both the child of a cousin marriage and went on to marry a cousin themselves — three, sometimes four consecutive generations of the same two family lines marrying back into each other, rather than a single instance diluted by outside blood.

And it wasn't left behind when the Sassanesi crossed the Atlantic. Sixty-three of these cousin marriages involve a bride or groom who eventually settled, married, or died in Chicago; another 45, though they took place back in Sassano, produced children who made the crossing themselves. All told, roughly one cousin marriage in ten connects to the Federal Street enclave.

A related pattern, where two brothers married two sisters from another family (or a brother and sister each married the other family's counterpart), is traced on the Notable records page. The children of those unions were double first cousins, and the tree contains one marriage between two of them: Francesco Ferro and Rosa Ferro, in 1895.

Some of the names will be familiar to anyone who has followed our Home page and Families list. Pietro Abbatemarco and Francesca Trotta, one of the earliest couples in our founding family records, were third cousins. Gabriele D'Amato and Maria Rosa Ricciardone were third cousins as well — a marriage that took some untangling, since Maria Rosa turned out to have married twice: first D'Amato in Sassano in 1889, then, evidently widowed, Michele Riccio in Chicago in 1898. Closer to home for this site's author: Francesco Cavallone and Rosa Libretti, married in Sassano on 22 Jan 1899, were first cousins once removed — Antonia Francesca di Bella was Francesco's grandmother and Rosa's great-grandmother. Francesco is the same great-grandfather whose long-sought birth record — and the brick wall it finally brought down — is described on the About page.

None of this is unusual for a town like Sassano, and we don't share it to cast any judgment on any family, ours included. Small, isolated communities the world over — mountain villages, island populations, tightly bound immigrant enclaves like the one on Federal Street — show this same pattern, for the same plain reason: the pool of people you were likely to meet, court, and marry was small, and church and family kept you close to those you already knew. For most of Sassano's history, marrying within one's own extended kinship network wasn't the exception. It was close to the rule.

It's worth a brief, honest word on what that can mean medically. Modern genetics tells us that closely related couples have a somewhat higher chance of passing the same rare gene to a child, nudging the risk of certain inherited conditions upward — for first cousins, roughly from a general-population baseline of 3–4% to something closer to 4–6%. The effect fades quickly with distance; second, third, and fourth cousins are genetically close to unrelated pairs. Where it can matter more is exactly the pattern we found here — the same two lines intermarrying across several generations running — which is one thread, among several including poverty, poor sanitation, and limited medical care, that may help explain the high rate of infant and child death visible elsewhere in these records. It is context, not a diagnosis of any one family or descendant.

Mostly, what the numbers tell us is a story we already knew in outline: for two centuries, Sassano was a place small enough that nearly everyone was, in some fashion, everyone else's cousin — and that closeness is written into nearly every family line that eventually found its way to Federal Street. The share has now settled at roughly one marriage in five, even as hundreds more individuals keep being added.

Everyone was kin.

If cousin marriage was common in Sassano, were the people who left for Chicago related too? We took the 159 people in the tree who were born in Sassano and died in Chicago and compared every possible pair of them, 12,561 pairs in all, looking for the nearest ancestor each pair shares. All 159 belong to a single connected family network, and 12,517 of the 12,561 pairs (99.6%) share a recorded ancestor. Every one of the 159 is related to at least one other; the 44 pairs with no recorded link are individual pairs, not individuals left out.

Most of the kinship is distant, as you would expect from a village of a few thousand people drawing on the same families for centuries. The typical pair are fourth or fifth cousins, which together account for about seven pairs in ten. Even so, roughly one pair in twelve is related as second cousins or closer, and 417 pairs are siblings, aunts and uncles, nieces and nephews, or first cousins.

The same holds for the people living today. We took the 358 US-born people, born 1930 or later, who have a Sassano-born ancestor in the tree and compared every pair. We also picked out the village's early surnames: the 42 names carried by ten or more Sassano-born people born before 1700, led by d'Amato, Trotta, Feminella, Ramondino, Florendino, Pellegrino, Ferro, Carlo and Calandriello. Every one of the 117 people in the group with an early surname is related to every other, including pairs with entirely different early names, such as a Trotta and a Feminella. The result is not special to old names, though: the other 241 people, whose surnames are later arrivals or come through the mother's line, are also related to everyone else.

So if your family has roots in Sassano, you are almost certainly a cousin of everyone else with Sassano roots. It is a distant kinship: the typical pair are fifth cousins, about four in five pairs fall between fourth and sixth cousins, and only one pair in ten is third cousins or closer. That is far from close family, and it should not be read as shared DNA, since cousins this distant share very little of it. It does mean that two Sassanesi families who meet in Chicago today can usually find a shared ancestor in the same few early lines, the same ones that kept marrying one another back in the village.

Two limits apply. A surname alone proves nothing, because a common name such as Russo can belong to unrelated families from other towns; it is the Sassano ancestry that links them. And the test covers only people already in the tree, so a family that left and was never recorded cannot be checked. Surnames in the comparison are each person's own, which for women may be a married name.

Widening the net to the 714 Sassano-born people with any Chicago residence or immigration record gives the same picture: 709 sit in the one network and 95% of pairs are related, with the remaining ten having no recorded relative among the group (several have no recorded parents). These figures measure what the tree records. A missing parent link breaks a connection on paper, so the true share of kin is likely higher.
