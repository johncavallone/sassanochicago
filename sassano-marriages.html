Anyone who has spent time in the parish books of San Giovanni Evangelista learns the same lesson we did while indexing them: in a town the size of Sassano, tucked in the hills 95 miles southeast of Naples, everyone eventually married a relative. Not out of scandal, but out of geography. For centuries the town's marriageable population was, quite literally, whoever lived within walking distance — and more often than not, that meant a cousin.

Using the full Sassano-Bompietro family tree — now over 31,512 individuals and 7,912 families as new records keep getting indexed — we set out to see just how common this really was, and whether it followed the Sassanesi all the way to Federal Street.

It did, and more than we expected.

Of the 6,369 marriages in the tree where both spouses' parentage could be traced, 1,444 were between blood relatives close enough to identify: first cousins, second cousins, third cousins, or fourth cousins, some with a generation's remove between them. The overall share rises to 22.67%, from 22.53% last update. Twelve cousin marriages joined the count and none left it, taking the total from 1,432 to 1,444, while the number of marriages checked rose from 6,356 to 6,369. All twelve are in families that were already in the tree. They surfaced because this update added 102 people to the tree and dropped 18 (a net gain of 84), 98 of the additions born before 1750, which carried several early lines back a generation and let existing couples be traced to a common ancestor. The ancestors behind the most new matches are Candeloro Calandriello (five marriages), Vito Orso Petrizzo (three) and Anna Maria Valvella (two). Six of the twelve are fourth cousins. Two are third cousins (Giovanni Antonio Petrizzo and Rosa Maria Villano, 10 Sep 1794, and Giovanni Agostino Villano and Bernardina Paolina Petrizzo, 2 Dec 1786), three are third cousins once removed (Michele Zozzaro and Giovanna Antonia Calandriello, 15 Nov 1803; Domenico Zozzaro and Arcangela Laveglia, 25 Jan 1798; Giovanni Caporrino and Raffaela Trotta, 17 Dec 1837), and one is a second-cousin-once-removed marriage, Sebastiano Nicola di Stavola and Rosa Pinto (6 May 1757), through Vito Orso Petrizzo. One couple already in the count moved to a closer relationship because the new links revealed a nearer ancestor: Giaccobbe Pasquale Cammarano and Barbara Videtta (25 Apr 1821) moved from third cousins once removed to third cousins. Fourth cousins rise to 507, third cousins once removed to 351, third cousins to 310 and second cousins once removed to 97. Second cousins stay at 102, second cousins twice removed at 13, first cousins at 34, first cousins once removed at 29 and first cousins twice removed at 1. The earliest marriages on record are unchanged. Multi-generational cases rise to 1,023.

What surprised us more was how often it repeated. 1,023 people in the tree are both the child of a cousin marriage and went on to marry a cousin themselves — three, sometimes four consecutive generations of the same two family lines marrying back into each other, rather than a single instance diluted by outside blood.

And it wasn't left behind when the Sassanesi crossed the Atlantic. Sixty-three of these cousin marriages involve a bride or groom who eventually settled, married, or died in Chicago; another 45, though they took place back in Sassano, produced children who made the crossing themselves. All told, roughly one cousin marriage in thirteen connects to the Federal Street enclave.

A related pattern, where two brothers married two sisters from another family (or a brother and sister each married the other family's counterpart), is traced on the Notable records page. The children of those unions were double first cousins, and the tree contains one marriage between two of them: Francesco Ferro and Rosa Ferro, in 1895.

Some of the names will be familiar to anyone who has followed our Home page and Families list. Pietro Abbatemarco and Francesca Trotta, one of the earliest couples in our founding family records, were third cousins. Gabriele D'Amato and Maria Rosa Ricciardone were third cousins as well — a marriage that took some untangling, since Maria Rosa turned out to have married twice: first D'Amato in Sassano in 1889, then Michele Riccio in Chicago in 1898. The first marriage ended in divorce rather than widowhood; Gabriele moved to New York and married Luisa Curcio in 1896. No record of the divorce itself has been found. Closer to home for this site's author: Francesco Cavallone and Rosa Libretti, married in Sassano on 22 Jan 1899, were first cousins once removed — Antonia Francesca di Bella was Francesco's grandmother and Rosa's great-grandmother. Francesco is the same great-grandfather whose long-sought birth record — and the brick wall it finally brought down — is described on the About page.

None of this is unusual for a town like Sassano, and we don't share it to cast any judgment on any family, ours included. Small, isolated communities the world over — mountain villages, island populations, tightly bound immigrant enclaves like the one on Federal Street — show this same pattern, for the same plain reason: the pool of people you were likely to meet, court, and marry was small, and church and family kept you close to those you already knew. For most of Sassano's history, marrying within one's own extended kinship network wasn't the exception. It was close to the rule.

It's worth a brief, honest word on what that can mean medically. Modern genetics tells us that closely related couples have a somewhat higher chance of passing the same rare gene to a child, nudging the risk of certain inherited conditions upward — for first cousins, roughly from a general-population baseline of 3–4% to something closer to 4–6%. The effect fades quickly with distance; second, third, and fourth cousins are genetically close to unrelated pairs. Where it can matter more is exactly the pattern we found here — the same two lines intermarrying across several generations running — which is one thread, among several including poverty, poor sanitation, and limited medical care, that may help explain the high rate of infant and child death visible elsewhere in these records. It is context, not a diagnosis of any one family or descendant.

Mostly, what the numbers tell us is a story we already knew in outline: for two centuries, Sassano was a place small enough that nearly everyone was, in some fashion, everyone else's cousin — and that closeness is written into nearly every family line that eventually found its way to Federal Street. The share has now edged past one marriage in five, even as hundreds more individuals keep being added.

160 Sassano-born, Chicago-died · 360 living-era descendants

Everyone was kin.

If cousin marriage was common in Sassano, were the people who left for Chicago related too? We took the 160 people in the tree who were born in Sassano and died in Chicago and compared every possible pair of them, 12,720 pairs in all, looking for the nearest ancestor each pair shares. All 160 belong to a single connected family network, and 12,698 of the 12,720 pairs (99.8%) share a recorded ancestor. Every one of the 160 is related to at least one other; the 22 pairs with no recorded link are individual pairs, not individuals left out.

Most of the kinship is distant, as you would expect from a village of a few thousand people drawing on the same families for centuries. The typical pair are fourth or fifth cousins, which together account for about seven pairs in ten. Even so, roughly one pair in twelve is related as second cousins or closer, and 418 pairs are siblings, aunts and uncles, nieces and nephews, or first cousins.

The same holds for the people living today. We took the 360 US-born people, born 1930 or later, who have a Sassano-born ancestor in the tree and compared every pair. We also picked out the village's early surnames: the 43 names carried by ten or more Sassano-born people born before 1700, led by d'Amato, Trotta, Feminella, Ramondino, Ferro, Florendino, Russo, Pellegrino and Carlo. Every one of the 152 people in the group with an early surname is related to every other, including pairs with entirely different early names, such as a Trotta and a Feminella. The result is not special to old names, though: the other 208 people, whose surnames are later arrivals or come through the mother's line, are also related to everyone else.

So if your family has roots in Sassano, you are almost certainly a cousin of everyone else with Sassano roots. Among the 360 living-era people, the typical pair are fifth cousins by their closest shared ancestor, about four in five pairs fall between fourth and sixth cousins, and only one pair in ten is third cousins or closer. It does mean that two Sassanesi families who meet in Chicago today can usually find a shared ancestor in the same few early lines, the same ones that kept marrying one another back in the village.

That cousin degree is only part of the story, and on its own it understates how much DNA these pairs may share. Degree follows the single closest shared ancestor. But because Sassano families married their cousins for generations, most pairs are connected through many ancestors at once: the same couples appear several times in each person's family tree, which geneticists call pedigree collapse, and every connection adds to the DNA two people are expected to share. We added up every documented path in the tree for every pair. For the typical pair of living-era people, the expected shared DNA is about five times what the closest ancestor alone would suggest, so a pair labelled fifth cousins behaves more like fourth-and-a-half cousins. About 85 percent of pairs share at least twice the single-path amount, and nearly one in five share ten times as much or more. The amounts are still small for the typical pair, around 8 centimorgans (cM) of expected shared DNA against under 2 cM on a closest-ancestor basis, but about 64% of pairs would be expected to reach the roughly 6 cM at which testing companies begin to report a match, against about 23% on the closest-ancestor basis. Among the 152 people with an early surname the numbers are higher still, a typical 10 cM.

This matters when reading a DNA match. In a community like this one, a match that looks like a second or third cousin from its shared centimorgans may be a more distant relative connected many times over, because the standard charts that convert shared cM into a relationship assume a single connection and so overstate how close the relationship is. Treat shared cM as a hint and let the documented tree decide the actual relationship. These are expected averages worked out from the paper tree; real inheritance is random, so any one pair can share much more or less, and the tree records only documented ancestors. Earlier ancestors that were never recorded would add more overlap, so true sharing is probably higher.

Two limits apply. A surname alone proves nothing, because a common name such as Russo can belong to unrelated families from other towns; it is the Sassano ancestry that links them. And the test covers only people already in the tree, so a family that left and was never recorded cannot be checked. Surnames in the comparison are each person's own, which for women may be a married name.

Widening the net to the 734 Sassano-born people with any Chicago residence or immigration record gives the same picture: 729 sit in the one network and 95% of pairs are related, while the other five sit outside it (several have no recorded parents). These figures measure what the tree records. A missing parent link breaks a connection on paper, so the true share of kin is likely higher.
