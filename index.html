The Story

A town small enough that everyone knew everyone.

Starting in the late 1890s, an area bounded by South 47th and 51st Streets along Armour Avenue — later renamed Federal Street — became home to hundreds of Italian immigrants from Sassano, a small town in the hills 95 miles southeast of Naples. Francesco Riccio, Nicola di Brizzi, Francesco Cavallone, his father-in-law Michele Libretti, and his brother Vincenzo Cavallone were among the first to arrive, between 1893 and 1898.

They weren't the first Italians in the neighborhood — families from Teggiano had settled nearby around 1885 — but parish marriage records suggest Cono Stavola, who married Maria Policastro on 12 February 1893, may have been the very first Sassanese to reach Chicago at all.

By the 1940 Census, Sassanesi families owned much of Federal Street between 47th and 50th. Many arrived as junk dealers and became grocers, restaurateurs, saloon owners, and sanitation workers — building, home by home, the life they couldn't have in Southern Italy after unification. Most married and baptized their children at Santa Maria Incoronata, the Riciglianesi parish on Alexander and Wentworth.

A firsthand account, 1924

What Federal Street actually looked like.

Most of what we know about daily life on Federal Street comes from family memory, census records, and city directories — useful, but distant. One document gets much closer: Alice Quan Rood's 1924 University of Chicago Master's dissertation, Social Conditions Among the Negroes on Federal Street Between Forty-Fifth Street and Fifty-Third Street, a firsthand social-work survey of the very blocks the Sassanesi called home, written while our great-grandparents' generation was still living there.

Rood's study was primarily about the neighborhood's African American residents, but she took the time to note the small Italian community living alongside them too — and what she found lines up closely with everything else this site documents about crowded, hardworking immigrant households. "Much over-crowding is found among the few Italian residents of Federal Street," she wrote, and the specific cases she recorded are worth sitting with.

Sixty-six years

How the neighborhood changed.

Federal Street didn't stay Federal Street forever — the community moved, rebuilt, and moved again.

1885

Teggiano arrives first

The Santoro, Gangone, and Lettiere families, from the neighboring town of Teggiano, settle in the Armour Avenue / Federal Street area before any family from Sassano.

1893

The first Sassanesi

Cono Stavola marries Maria Policastro and settles near 30th and Wells. Within five years, Riccio, di Brizzi, Libretti, and Cavallone families establish Federal Street as the heart of the enclave.

1940

A neighborhood of homeowners

Census records show Sassanesi families owning much of Federal Street between 47th and 50th — grocers, restaurateurs, club owners, and tradesmen investing in the community they built.

1949–59

Displacement, and a new center

The Chicago Housing Authority acquires the Federal Street properties to build the Robert Taylor Homes. Families scatter southwest; a smaller enclave takes root at 69th and Hermitage.

1960s

One more wave

69th and Hermitage welcomes a fresh wave of Sassanese immigrants — the di Miele, di Brizzi, and other families — closing out nearly seven decades of continuous migration.
